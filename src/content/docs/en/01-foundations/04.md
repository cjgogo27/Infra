## The engineering problem

The service generates more tokens per second, yet users find it slower. Larger batches have increased waiting time.

> Keep this question in mind: What must accompany a claim of 30% improvement?

**Latency · Throughput · Time to first token (TTFT) · Time per output token (TPOT)**

> **In one sentence: Throughput measures work completed per interval; latency measures how long one item waits.**

Training often reports samples/s, tokens/s, and step time. Serving also needs request throughput, first-token latency, and completion time. TTFT usually includes queueing and execution before the first token. TPOT is often (completion time−TTFT)/(output tokens−1), requiring at least two output tokens; state your exact convention.

Teaching example: A processes one request in 100 ms. B processes four in 160 ms. Ignoring other overhead, throughput is 10 versus 25 requests/s, while B has longer per-request execution latency. Throughput gains do not establish lower latency.

Fix hardware, versions, precision, input/output lengths, concurrency, and quality criteria. Separate warm-up from steady state and report p50, p95, and sample count. A few dozen samples do not reliably estimate tail latency. Distinguish client wall-clock time from GPU device time.

Amdahl's law gives an overall speedup of 1/((1−p)+p/s) when fraction p is accelerated by s. If model computation accounts for 40% and becomes twice as fast, the whole system improves by only 1.25×. This assumes the rest stays unchanged; queueing and overlap complicate reality.

**Upstream and downstream:** Batch systems may wait to form batches; closed-loop control emphasizes tail latency and fresh observations.

**LabBot in practice:** Measure both model execution and camera-exposure-to-command time. Continue decomposing the gap.
